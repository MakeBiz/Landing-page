Sales forecasting from CRM data

A forecast based on gut feel misses actuals every month. If your CRM holds amounts, stages and dates, you can forecast from data and check it every week.

A CRM sales forecast combines the value of open deals, the probability of each stage based on past closes, and the payment date adjusted for your average sales cycle. In MakeBiz BI the monthly and quarterly forecast is part of the Standard plan at AED 700 a month, and the first 14 days are free.

MakeBiz Technologies · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

A forecast is built from three numbers: amount, probability and date

Every open deal in your CRM has an amount, a stage and an expected close date. Those three fields are what a forecast is made of. The amount tells you what you could earn. The probability tells you how likely it is. The date tells you which month the money lands in.

The simplest formula multiplies each deal amount by its stage probability and adds the results up by month: a weighted pipeline. A deal worth AED 40,000 at a stage with a 25% probability contributes AED 10,000 to the forecast. No single deal pays exactly that, but across many deals the errors cancel out.

The weak spot is data quality. A blank amount drops the deal out, and a close date that has said "tomorrow" for three months keeps the forecast sliding. So the data comes first, more on that below.

Stage probability comes from history, not gut feel

In many portals stage probabilities were typed in during setup: 10, 30, 50, 90%. Those are guesses. A real probability comes from closed deals over the last 6 to 12 months: of all deals that reached a stage, how many ended up paying.

Recalculate probabilities every quarter and separately for each line of business. A repeat order and a first deal with a new client behave differently at the same stage, and so may separate free zone and mainland teams.

The sales cycle moves the forecast between months

Reps set close dates optimistically, so the deal date is compared with the average cycle from each stage to payment. If deals at "Quote sent" typically close 35 days later and a rep has set a date one week out, the forecast uses the calculated date instead.

The second rule covers stalled deals. A deal that has been sitting at a stage two or three times longer than average is very unlikely to close on time. Those deals go onto a separate list for the sales manager and carry less weight in the forecast. You can hand stalled-deal control to the AI sales manager: the agent watches the pipeline, nudges the rep and alerts the manager if nothing happens.

Talk about forecasting

Run the weighted pipeline and the rep forecast side by side

Stage maths cannot know what the rep knows: the client has signed internally, but payment will clear after quarter end. That is why a rep forecast runs alongside the weighted pipeline. In Bitrix24 we add a deal field with three values:

  • Commit: the deal will close this period and the rep stands behind it
  • Upside: there is a real chance, but open questions remain
  • Not this period: the deal is alive, but the money will come later

The sales director looks at both. If the rep forecast is far above the weighted pipeline, the team is overrating its deals; far below, and deals may be stuck. The weekly meeting turns to specific deals instead of impressions.

Calls are another source of truth. What a client says about timing and budget often never makes it into the deal record. Clean CRM data comes before the forecast

A forecast inherits every error in your CRM. The minimum checklist before you start:

  1. Every open deal has an amount in AED and a close date
  2. Stages have exit criteria: what has to happen for a deal to move on
  3. No duplicate deals for the same client and the same need
  4. Lost deals are closed with a reason instead of lingering in the pipeline
  5. Deal amount and invoice amount are calculated the same way, with or without 5% VAT

Questions and answers

How do I calculate the probability of a deal stage?

Take closed deals from the last 6 to 12 months and work out what share of deals that reached the stage ended up paying. That share is the probability, and it should be recalculated every quarter.

What is a weighted pipeline?

It is the total of all open deals, each multiplied by the probability of its stage. It shows expected revenue by month, allowing for the fact that some deals will not close.

How much data do I need for a forecast?

You need a history of closed deals, both won and lost, with amounts and dates. The more deals per period, the more stable the probabilities. With only a few deals, the rep forecast matters more than the maths.
